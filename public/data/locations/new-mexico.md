## Section 125 cafeteria plans for New Mexico employers

New Mexico employers compete for workers across a landscape that few other states share: federal research labs and military installations, two distinct oil and gas basins, a fast-growing film and television industry, and a deep network of pueblos, tribal nations, rural school districts, and community clinics. A [Section 125 cafeteria plan](/services/employers) gives employers in all of those settings a practical recruiting and retention tool, letting their people pay for real benefits with pre-tax dollars and stretch a paycheck further without the employer raising base pay.

That matters in a state where many employers run lean. A well administered cafeteria plan lets an employee direct part of gross pay toward qualified coverage before taxes are calculated, so the same salary supports more usable benefits. For the employer, offering pre-tax benefits is a recruiting advantage that costs far less than an across-the-board raise, and the lower payroll base does trim the employer's matching FICA on participating wages as a rolled-in effect rather than the reason to offer the plan.

## The New Mexico economy a plan has to fit

New Mexico's employer base is unusually varied, and a cafeteria plan has to work for each part of it:

- **Federal labs, defense, and aerospace.** Sandia National Laboratories and Kirtland Air Force Base in Albuquerque, Los Alamos National Laboratory in the Jemez Mountains, White Sands Missile Range, and Spaceport America near Truth or Consequences anchor a large science and defense workforce, along with the contractors and support firms that orbit them.
- **Oil and gas.** The Permian Basin in the southeast, centered on Lea and Eddy counties around Hobbs and Carlsbad, and the San Juan Basin in the northwest around Farmington, employ field crews, drivers, and service companies whose rosters mix hourly and salaried staff.
- **Film and television production.** Albuquerque and Santa Fe host major studio operations and a steady stream of productions, supporting crews, vendors, and post-production employers across the state.
- **Healthcare.** Hospital systems and clinics in Albuquerque, Las Cruces, Santa Fe, Rio Rancho, and the rural counties are among the largest employers in their communities.
- **Education and public sector.** The University of New Mexico, New Mexico State University in Las Cruces, and dozens of rural and reservation school districts run large, benefits-eligible payrolls.
- **Agriculture and food.** Dairy in the eastern plains, pecans and chile in the Mesilla Valley, and ranching across the state sustain seasonal and year-round employers.
- **Tribal and pueblo employers.** The Navajo Nation, the nineteen Pueblos, and the Mescalero and Jicarilla Apache nations operate enterprises, gaming, healthcare, and government services that employ thousands.

## Why the plan structure suits these employers

Each of these sectors shares a common challenge: keeping benefits competitive while controlling cost. A cafeteria plan lets a Carlsbad oilfield service company, a Las Cruces clinic, a Rio Rancho call center, or a rural school district offer the same pre-tax structure that larger urban employers use, without building benefits administration in house. ## What can run pre-tax

A cafeteria plan covers a defined set of qualified benefits. Common pre-tax elections include health, dental, and vision insurance premiums, health flexible spending accounts and dependent care FSAs, health savings account contributions, and dependent care assistance. Most group benefits that protect health or care obligations qualify, while items such as long-term care insurance fall outside Section 125 and are handled separately.

## Geography that shapes the work

New Mexico's distances are part of the job. Albuquerque and Bernalillo County form the population center along the Rio Grande corridor, with Santa Fe to the north as the capital and Las Cruces and Doña Ana County anchoring the south near the El Paso border region. The Four Corners in the northwest, where New Mexico meets Arizona, Colorado, and Utah, centers on San Juan County. The southeastern Permian counties, the eastern dairy plains around Clovis and Portales, and the mountain communities of the north each run on their own employer mix. A statewide administrator has to coordinate payroll for employers spread across all of it.
