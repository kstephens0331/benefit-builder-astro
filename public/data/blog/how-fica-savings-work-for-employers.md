A Section 125 plan changes an employer's payroll taxes in a specific way that is worth understanding precisely. The effect comes from FICA, the federal payroll tax that funds Social Security and Medicare. This article walks through exactly how a pre-tax benefit shrinks the FICA wage base, and what that means for your payroll.

## The 7.65% employer share

FICA is split into two taxes. Social Security is 6.2% of wages up to an annual wage base, and Medicare is 1.45% of all wages. Added together that is 7.65%. Both the employee and the employer pay that 7.65%, so on a normal payroll the government collects 15.3% of wages in FICA before income tax is even considered.

The employer share is the part a Section 125 plan affects. Every dollar of wages removed from the FICA wage base removes 7.65 cents of employer payroll tax.

## How a pre-tax election removes wages from the base

When an employee makes a pre-tax election under a Section 125 plan, that amount is a salary reduction. It is excluded from the wages used to calculate FICA. The employee no longer pays their 7.65% on it, and you no longer pay your 7.65% on it.

Consider a simple example. An employee elects 200 dollars a month in pre-tax benefits, which is 2,400 dollars a year. At the 7.65% employer rate, that single employee lowers your payroll tax by about 184 dollars a year. Multiply that across a participating group and the numbers add up. ## The effect scales with participation

Because the effect comes from each participating employee, the total grows with enrollment. This is why clear employee communication matters. A plan that is well explained gets higher participation, and higher participation means a larger effect for the employer. A plan that is poorly communicated leaves that benefit underused.

It is also why the employer and employee interests line up. The same pre-tax election that raises an employee's take-home pay lowers the employer's payroll tax. Both sides gain on the same dollar.

## What the effect does and does not cover

The employer FICA effect is real, but it is not unlimited. Social Security tax only applies up to the annual wage base, so for very high earners the Social Security portion may already be maxed out and only the 1.45% Medicare portion is reduced on additional pre-tax dollars. For the large majority of employees, the full 7.65% applies.

It also depends on the benefit being a qualified pre-tax benefit under Section 125 and on the plan being properly documented and tested. Pre-tax treatment that is not backed by a compliant plan is not defensible. Our [compliance overview](/compliance) covers what a valid plan requires.

## Turning this into a decision

For most employers the practical question is whether the value the plan delivers covers the cost of running it. For a typical group it does, because the administration fee is a small percentage while the pre-tax effect applies to every qualified dollar across the whole group. The benefit to your team, a stronger and more competitive offering, is the reason most employers put a plan in place.
